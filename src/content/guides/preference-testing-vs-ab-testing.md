Both tests compare versions of something, such as a design, an ad, a thumbnail or a headline. A preference test asks people which version they prefer. An A/B test shows each version to different people in real use and measures what they do. They answer different questions, and they work best one after the other.

## What a preference test is

People see two or more versions and choose the one they prefer, usually in answer to a question such as "Which would you click?" You can run it before anything is built or published, with a mockup, an exported image or a rough cut of a video. It takes a few dozen people and a day or two.

A preference test measures what people say when they look on purpose. That is useful, but it is not the same as what they do.

## What an A/B test is

An A/B test splits real traffic. Some people see version A, others see version B, and you compare a result such as clicks, sign-ups or sales. Ad platforms, website testing tools and YouTube's [test and compare for titles and thumbnails](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/16391400) all work this way.

An A/B test measures behaviour, which is what you care about in the end. It needs the versions to be live, enough traffic to tell a real difference from chance, and time.

## The difference at a glance

| | Preference test | A/B test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Question it answers | Which version do people prefer? | Which version makes people act? |
| What it measures | Stated preference | Behaviour in real use |
| What you need | The versions as images or videos, and a few dozen people | Live versions and enough traffic |
| How many versions | Many. Comparing in pairs keeps a long list manageable | Usually two to four, because each one takes a share of the traffic |
| How long it takes | Hours to a few days | Days to weeks, depending on traffic |
| Main weakness | People judge more carefully than they would while scrolling | Weak versions get real traffic, and low traffic gives noisy results |

## When to use a preference test

- Before you build or publish, while changes are still cheap.
- When you have many options and want to drop the weak ones.
- When you have no traffic yet: a new product, a new channel, a video that isn't out.
- When the choice is about first impressions, such as a thumbnail, a cover, a logo or a product photo.

## When to use an A/B test

- When the versions are live and you have enough traffic to split.
- When the result you care about is an action: a click, a sign-up or a sale.
- When people can't predict their own behaviour, as with prices, form length or the order of a page.

## How to use both

1. Make several versions that differ in one thing, such as the image, the headline or the opening of a video.
2. Run a preference test with people like your buyers or viewers, and drop the versions that lose.
3. Run an A/B test on the two or three that remain.
4. Keep the winner, note what made it win, and use that in the next round.

An A/B test splits traffic between versions, so every extra version needs more traffic and time. Cutting the list first means the live test spends traffic only on serious options. [How to test ads before spending](/guides/how-to-test-ads-before-spending) walks through this for ads.

If the two tests disagree, trust the A/B test for the result you measured. The preference test still tells you what people like when they look closely, and that is worth knowing for the next version.

## Common mistakes

- Treating a preference test as proof of behaviour. "I would click this" is not a click.
- Calling a small lead a win. With few voters or little traffic, a narrow gap is often chance. [How many voters do you need?](/guides/how-many-voters-do-you-need) shows how much.
- Asking the wrong people. Your team knows the product and judges it differently from strangers.
- Changing everything between versions. If the image, the copy and the layout all differ, a winner teaches you nothing you can reuse.
